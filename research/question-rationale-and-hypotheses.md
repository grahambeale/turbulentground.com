# AI shift research study

## Question rationale, interpretation and hypotheses

**Version:** 1.0  
**Date:** 28 August 2026  
**Instrument:** 24 statements across 12 paired themes, plus four optional context questions and one optional open comment

## Purpose of this document

This document explains:

- why each question is included;
- what the response may indicate;
- how each question differs from its paired question;
- what patterns the study may be able to identify;
- what the data cannot establish on its own; and
- the hypotheses that can be tested or challenged as the dataset grows.

It is an interpretation guide, not a scoring manual or a claim that the survey is a validated psychological instrument.

## Research purpose

The study explores how people working in or alongside product development teams experience the shift to AI-assisted work. It focuses on two related but distinct perspectives:

1. **Your contribution:** what a participant reports doing, choosing or influencing.
2. **Conditions around you:** what a participant reports being enabled, expected or given by colleagues, teams and organisations.

The central research question is not simply whether people use AI. It is whether product teams and their wider organisations are creating the human and organisational conditions needed to use AI with judgement, agency and care.

The paired design is intended to reveal tensions. A participant may report strong personal practice but weak surrounding conditions, or supportive conditions but limited personal action. Those gaps may be more informative than either response alone.

## How to interpret responses

Each statement uses a five-point agreement scale:

- 1: Strongly disagree
- 2: Disagree
- 3: Neither agree nor disagree
- 4: Agree
- 5: Strongly agree
- Not applicable
- Prefer not to say

Responses are ordinal. The difference between 1 and 2 cannot automatically be assumed to be psychologically identical to the difference between 4 and 5. Means can be used as a readable summary, but distributions, medians and the proportion choosing each response should also be examined.

“Not applicable” and “Prefer not to say” are analytically different. The first may indicate that a question does not fit the person’s work. The second records a choice not to disclose. Neither should be converted to a neutral score.

## The 12 paired themes

### 1. Judgement

#### Question A

> When AI and I disagree, I rely on my own judgement.

**Why it is asked**  
This tests reported willingness to retain human agency when an AI recommendation conflicts with the participant’s own conclusion.

**What the data may indicate**  
A higher response may indicate confidence in exercising independent judgement. A lower response may indicate deference to AI, uncertainty about personal expertise, or a belief that the AI is usually more reliable.

**What it cannot establish**  
It does not show whether the participant’s judgement is actually correct, whether rejecting AI is appropriate, or how often disagreement occurs.

#### Question B

> I can recognise when AI output is wrong.

**Why it is asked**  
Independent judgement is only protective when a person believes they can identify inaccurate, unsuitable or misleading output.

**What the data may indicate**  
It may indicate perceived AI literacy, relevant domain knowledge or confidence in evaluating output.

**Why it is distinct**  
Question A is about what a person says they do during disagreement. Question B is about whether they believe they can detect a problem in the first place. Someone may be willing to challenge AI but lack confidence in spotting errors, or recognise errors but still defer to AI.

**Interpretation caution**  
Question B is currently stored in the “conditions” lens, but its wording primarily measures perceived individual capability. It should not be described as an organisational condition without further justification or a wording change.

### 2. Time and workload

#### Question A

> When AI saves me time, I can use that time to improve my work.

**Why it is asked**  
AI productivity gains can be used to improve quality or absorbed into higher output expectations. This asks whether the working environment leaves room for quality improvement.

**What the data may indicate**  
A higher response may indicate that saved time is available for refinement, checking, thinking or craft. A lower response may indicate workload intensification or pressure to convert every efficiency into more output.

#### Question B

> I can use some of the time AI saves me for myself, rather than taking on more work.

**Why it is asked**  
This asks whether the participant personally protects any of the capacity created by AI.

**What the data may indicate**  
It may indicate boundary-setting, personal control over workload, or the practical ability to resist immediate task replacement.

**Why it is distinct**  
Question A asks whether the environment permits saved time to support quality. Question B asks whether the individual actively preserves some of that time. The gap may distinguish organisational pressure from personal boundary-setting.

### 3. Working relationships

#### Question A

> I talk to colleagues directly instead of relying on AI summaries of their work.

**Why it is asked**  
AI can make information easier to consume while reducing direct contact with the people whose work or experience is being summarised.

**What the data may indicate**  
A higher response may indicate an active effort to preserve direct communication, context and interpersonal understanding.

#### Question B

> My colleagues speak with me directly instead of relying on AI summaries of my work.

**Why it is asked**  
Working relationships are reciprocal. A person can seek direct contact while still feeling that others understand them mainly through generated summaries.

**What the data may indicate**  
It may indicate whether the participant feels heard directly and whether their working environment preserves person-to-person understanding.

**Why it is distinct**  
Question A measures the participant’s reported behaviour towards colleagues. Question B measures their experience of colleagues’ behaviour towards them. Together they can reveal an asymmetry in perceived relational effort.

### 4. Skill and craft

#### Question A

> My organisation allows enough time to check the quality of AI-assisted work.

**Why it is asked**  
Responsible AI use depends on whether deadlines and expectations allow output to be checked and improved.

**What the data may indicate**  
A higher response may indicate that quality assurance is supported in practice. A lower response may indicate that speed expectations compete with review and professional standards.

#### Question B

> I check AI-assisted work as carefully as my other work.

**Why it is asked**  
Available time does not guarantee careful checking. This asks about the participant’s own reported standard of care.

**What the data may indicate**  
It may indicate consistency of quality standards across AI-assisted and non-AI work.

**Why it is distinct**  
Question A asks whether checking is enabled. Question B asks whether the participant reports doing it. High personal care alongside low organisational time may identify hidden effort or an unsustainable quality burden.

### 5. Autonomy

#### Question A

> I’m comfortable explaining how I use AI at work.

**Why it is asked**  
Openly accounting for AI use may reflect confidence, transparency and the absence of shame or concealment.

**What the data may indicate**  
A higher response may indicate comfort describing when and how AI contributes to work. A lower response could reflect uncertainty, fear of judgement, unclear policy or use the participant would rather not disclose.

#### Question B

> My organisation trusts me to decide when to rely on AI output.

**Why it is asked**  
This examines whether the organisation trusts the participant to exercise judgement about when AI output is appropriate to use.

**What the data may indicate**  
It may indicate discretion, organisational trust or the degree of managerial control surrounding AI-assisted work.

**Why it is distinct**  
Question A concerns explainability and openness. Question B concerns trusted judgement. A person may be transparent about AI use without feeling trusted to decide when its output is reliable, or feel trusted while still being uncomfortable discussing how they use it.

**Interpretation caution**  
High trust is not automatically evidence of safe practice. Appropriate oversight may be necessary in high-risk work. The response should be interpreted alongside policy clarity, accountability and the nature of the participant’s role.

### 6. Learning

#### Question A

> I have opportunities at work to build skills that will remain useful as AI changes my work.

**Why it is asked**  
Organisations may benefit from current AI productivity without investing in employees’ longer-term development.

**What the data may indicate**  
A higher response may indicate access to development opportunities the participant expects to remain useful as their work changes. A lower response may indicate that AI is covering capability gaps while opportunities for continued learning narrow.

#### Question B

> I actively develop skills that will remain useful as AI changes my work.

**Why it is asked**  
Opportunity and action are not the same. This asks whether the participant reports taking responsibility for continued development.

**What the data may indicate**  
It may indicate active learning, adaptation and investment in capabilities the participant expects to remain useful as work changes.

**Why it is distinct**  
Question A measures perceived opportunity supplied by work. Question B measures reported personal action. Their difference may show whether development is blocked by the environment or available but not taken up.

**Interpretation caution**  
“Skills that will remain useful” still involves personal expectation, but it does not require participants to claim that any skill is permanently AI-proof. Responses indicate present opportunity and behaviour, not an objective forecast of future labour-market value.

**Instrument version note**

The Learning wording above begins with `phase3-v2-2026-09-06`. Earlier stored responses are labelled `phase3-v1`. Analyse or compare the versions explicitly rather than silently treating these two wordings as identical.

### 7. Trust

#### Question A

> I trust my colleagues to decide when to rely on AI.

**Why it is asked**  
AI adoption changes not only whether people are trusted, but whether they extend professional discretion to others.

**What the data may indicate**  
A higher response may indicate confidence in colleagues’ judgement. A lower response may indicate concern about capability, standards or inappropriate reliance on AI.

#### Question B

> I’m trusted to decide when to rely on AI output.

**Why it is asked**  
This measures the trust the participant feels they receive from others.

**What the data may indicate**  
It may indicate experienced discretion and confidence from managers or colleagues.

**Why it is distinct**  
Question A measures trust given. Question B measures trust received. A gap may reveal uneven or asymmetric trust within a team.

### 8. Being seen

#### Question A

> I give colleagues full credit when AI helps them work faster.

**Why it is asked**  
AI may alter how people attribute effort, expertise and ownership. This asks whether AI assistance reduces the credit a participant gives others.

**What the data may indicate**  
A higher response may indicate that the participant continues to recognise human contribution when AI is involved.

#### Question B

> I receive credit for the judgement I add to AI-assisted work.

**Why it is asked**  
Valuable human work may become less visible when the final output appears to have been generated quickly.

**What the data may indicate**  
It may indicate whether the participant feels their judgement, editing and responsibility are recognised.

**Why it is distinct**  
Question A measures recognition given to others. Question B measures recognition experienced by the participant. A gap may expose a perceived imbalance between generosity towards others and recognition received.

### 9. Meaning

#### Question A

> As AI changes my tasks, I look for new ways to make my work meaningful.

**Why it is asked**  
When routine tasks change or disappear, individuals may need to actively reconnect their role with work that matters to them.

**What the data may indicate**  
A higher response may indicate active meaning-making or efforts to reshape work.

#### Question B

> The work I do alongside AI feels meaningful.

**Why it is asked**  
Active effort to find meaning does not guarantee that current work feels meaningful.

**What the data may indicate**  
It may indicate the participant’s present sense of purpose or value in AI-assisted work.

**Why it is distinct**  
Question A measures reported personal action. Question B measures the experienced outcome. High seeking with low experienced meaning may suggest that personal effort is not resolving a wider problem in job design.

### 10. Pace

#### Question A

> The pace expected of me when using AI is sustainable.

**Why it is asked**  
When AI makes faster output possible, organisations may reset expectations around that maximum speed.

**What the data may indicate**  
A higher response may indicate manageable external expectations. A lower response may indicate intensification, fatigue risk or an inability to maintain the expected pace.

#### Question B

> I set a sustainable pace when I work with AI.

**Why it is asked**  
This asks whether the participant personally regulates their working pace.

**What the data may indicate**  
It may indicate boundary-setting or control over tempo.

**Why it is distinct**  
Question A measures expected pace. Question B measures pace set by the participant. A high personal score with a low conditions score may identify resistance to pressure, hidden overtime or a conflict between personal practice and organisational expectations.

### 11. AI in team decisions

#### Question A

> My organisation clearly explains how AI should be used.

**Why it is asked**  
People need understandable guidance to make consistent decisions about appropriate AI use.

**What the data may indicate**  
A higher response may indicate policy clarity and practical guidance. A lower response may indicate ambiguity, inconsistent norms or a gap between formal policy and usable direction.

#### Question B

> I influence how my team uses AI.

**Why it is asked**  
Clear rules do not show whether people have a voice in shaping team practices.

**What the data may indicate**  
It may indicate participation, agency and local influence over adoption.

**Why it is distinct**  
Question A concerns clarity supplied by the organisation. Question B concerns influence exercised by the participant. Together they distinguish being informed from being involved.

### 12. Job security

#### Question A

> I feel secure in my role as AI changes my work.

**Why it is asked**  
AI adoption may affect people’s sense of longer-term role stability even before any formal job change occurs.

**What the data may indicate**  
A higher response may indicate perceived security. A lower response may indicate uncertainty or threat associated with AI-related change.

#### Question B

> I am preparing for ways AI could change my role.

**Why it is asked**  
Feeling secure and preparing for change are separate. Preparation may occur because someone feels threatened, because they feel optimistic, or because they routinely plan ahead.

**What the data may indicate**  
It may indicate reported adaptation, planning or career preparation.

**Why it is distinct**  
Question A measures felt security. Question B measures reported action. Preparation must not automatically be interpreted as confidence or insecurity. The relationship between the two responses is the useful signal.

## Context questions

All context questions are optional. They support subgroup exploration rather than evaluation of individuals.

### Current role

This may show whether experiences differ by organisational seniority or managerial responsibility. For example, leaders may report more influence over AI practices but may perceive pace, trust or policy differently from individual contributors.

It must not be treated as a precise hierarchy. “Team lead or manager” and “Head of function or above” can mean very different things across organisations.

### Discipline

This may show whether AI is experienced differently across product management, design, engineering, research, delivery, data, growth and operations.

Small subgroup sizes create a high risk of unstable or identifying comparisons. Categories should only be reported when there are enough responses to protect privacy and support a credible comparison.

### Responsibility for how a team builds or ships product

This separates formal role labels from practical responsibility. It may help identify whether decision-making responsibility is associated with greater influence, workload pressure or accountability for AI-assisted work.

### Organisation size

This may indicate whether policy clarity, autonomy, learning opportunities or working pace vary with organisational scale.

Organisation size is not a proxy for organisational maturity. Any observed difference will need qualitative explanation before it becomes a recommendation.

## Open comment

> Is there anything else you’d like to tell me about how AI is affecting your work?

The open comment gives participants a way to identify experiences the fixed statements miss, explain apparently contradictory answers, or introduce new themes.

Comments can be coded for recurring patterns, exceptions and language used by participants. They should not be used merely as quotations that decorate a quantitative conclusion. They may challenge the structure of the survey itself and suggest changes or deeper qualitative studies.

## What the study may be able to establish

With an adequate and sufficiently varied sample, the study may identify:

- which themes participants rate most and least positively;
- where reported personal contribution and surrounding conditions diverge;
- whether particular tensions recur across participants;
- whether patterns differ by role, discipline, product responsibility or organisation size;
- whether some themes tend to move together;
- how the benchmark changes as more invited participants respond;
- which topics require interviews, case studies or dedicated follow-up research; and
- how participants describe consequences that were not anticipated by the instrument.

The study may generate useful evidence about this invited sample. It cannot, without an appropriate sampling strategy, establish prevalence across all product professionals or all organisations.

## What the study cannot establish on its own

The survey cannot by itself establish:

- that AI caused a reported experience;
- that a participant’s organisation objectively has a particular quality;
- that self-reported behaviour matches observed behaviour;
- that high agreement is always desirable;
- that one participant or organisation is psychologically healthy or unhealthy;
- that a difference between subgroups is meaningful when samples are small;
- that correlated themes have a causal relationship;
- that the 12 themes form one valid overall score; or
- that the current benchmark represents the wider workforce.

The instrument currently uses one item for each side of each theme. Single items are accessible and reduce effort, but they provide limited evidence about reliability. The paired themes should therefore be described as descriptive indicators, not validated scales.

## Analysis principles

1. Keep contribution and conditions separate. Do not collapse all 24 responses into one overall score.
2. Treat the benchmark as a changing description of the invited study sample, not a target or norm.
3. Report distributions alongside means wherever space permits.
4. Preserve “Not applicable” and “Prefer not to say” as separate missing-response categories.
5. State subgroup sizes internally and suppress public subgroup reporting where privacy or stability is at risk.
6. Examine paired gaps within themes, but do not treat the mathematical gap as a validated psychological measure.
7. Look for disconfirming comments and outliers, not only dominant patterns.
8. Separate findings discovered in the data from hypotheses specified before analysis.
9. Use careful language such as “participants reported” and “within this invited sample.”
10. Use follow-up interviews or focused studies to investigate mechanisms before making causal recommendations.

## Hypotheses to test and challenge

These are provisional, falsifiable expectations. They are prompts for analysis, not claims the study is designed to confirm.

### Personal contribution and organisational conditions

**H1. Personal practice will be rated more positively than surrounding conditions across most themes.**  
Challenge by comparing the paired response distribution within every theme, not only the overall means.

**H2. The largest contribution-to-conditions gaps will occur in time and workload, skill and craft, pace, and AI in team decisions.**  
Challenge by ranking the absolute paired gap for all 12 themes and examining uncertainty rather than selecting only expected examples.

**H3. Some participants will report responsible personal AI practice while also reporting that their organisation does not provide enough time, clarity or sustainable expectations.**  
Challenge by testing whether high contribution responses actually coexist with low condition responses within the same participant.

**H4. Supportive conditions will not always produce strong personal action.**  
Challenge by identifying participants with high condition responses and neutral or low contribution responses, then using comments or follow-up interviews to explore why.

### Judgement, trust and oversight

**H5. Confidence in recognising wrong AI suggestions will be positively associated with relying on personal judgement during disagreement.**  
Challenge by cross-tabulating the two judgement items and actively examining cases where confidence and action diverge.

**H6. Trust received will be positively associated with comfort explaining AI use.**  
Challenge by checking whether people can feel trusted while still concealing or feeling uncomfortable discussing their AI use.

**H7. Close monitoring will be associated with lower reported trust and lower influence over team AI practices.**  
Challenge by considering whether appropriate oversight in higher-risk roles produces close monitoring without low trust.

**H8. Clear organisational guidance will be associated with greater felt trust, not less autonomy.**  
Challenge by testing whether clarity and discretion move together, and by looking for organisations where rules are experienced as control.

### Time, quality and pace

**H9. Participants who cannot use saved time to improve quality will also report less sustainable pace expectations.**  
Challenge by comparing the time-and-workload conditions item with the pace conditions item.

**H10. AI time savings will often be absorbed by new tasks rather than protected for reflection or recovery.**  
Challenge by examining the full distribution of the personal time-protection item and looking for disciplines or organisations where time is consistently retained.

**H11. Reported checking standards will remain high even where organisations do not allow enough checking time.**  
Challenge by testing for the predicted high-contribution, low-condition pairing and investigating whether this represents hidden effort, overconfidence or genuinely efficient checking.

**H12. Unsustainable pace will be more strongly associated with organisational expectations than with an individual’s inability to regulate pace.**  
Challenge by comparing the two pace items and resisting a causal conclusion from correlation alone.

### Relationships, recognition and meaning

**H13. Direct conversations will remain common, but participants will report giving more relational effort than they receive.**  
Challenge by comparing the two working-relationship items and testing whether the apparent asymmetry is consistent or merely a self-report bias.

**H14. Participants will report giving colleagues more credit than they feel they receive for AI-assisted work.**  
Challenge by comparing the paired “being seen” items and considering social desirability in self-reported generosity.

**H15. Seeking meaningful work will be rated more positively than experiencing current AI-assisted work as meaningful.**  
Challenge by comparing action and experience within the meaning theme.

**H16. Lower recognition will be associated with lower meaning in AI-assisted work.**  
Challenge by testing the relationship across participants while considering role, discipline and job security as alternative explanations.

**H17. Greater reliance on AI summaries will be associated with lower reported recognition or meaning.**  
Challenge through quantitative association and qualitative comments. The survey cannot establish whether summaries caused the difference.

### Learning and job change

**H18. Participants will report more personal effort to develop durable skills than opportunities supplied by their organisation.**  
Challenge by comparing the learning pair and identifying settings where organisational opportunity exceeds personal action.

**H19. Preparation for role change will not have a simple relationship with job security.**  
Challenge by testing for both possibilities: preparation may rise with insecurity, or it may rise with confidence and opportunity.

**H20. Participants with more learning opportunities will report greater job security and meaning.**  
Challenge by examining whether the association survives role, discipline and organisation-size comparisons, while avoiding causal claims.

### Differences between groups

**H21. People with responsibility for how teams build or ship product will report more influence over AI practices than those without that responsibility.**  
Challenge by comparing distributions and identifying informal influence among people without formal responsibility.

**H22. Senior leaders will report clearer organisational AI guidance and more sustainable conditions than individual contributors.**  
Challenge by directly comparing perceptions. A disagreement between seniority groups may itself be an important blind spot.

**H23. Individual contributors will report lower influence and lower recognition than managers or senior leaders.**  
Challenge by checking each outcome independently and avoiding a single “seniority effect.”

**H24. Disciplines will differ in judgement confidence, checking conditions and perceived job security.**  
Challenge only once subgroup sizes are adequate. Treat any early differences as exploratory and unstable.

**H25. Larger organisations will report clearer guidance but less day-to-day autonomy.**  
Challenge by comparing organisation-size groups while recognising that size does not measure governance maturity or risk exposure.

### Instrument and response behaviour

**H26. “Prefer not to say” responses will be more common for job security than for practical workflow questions.**  
Challenge by comparing non-response choices by item.

**H27. “Not applicable” responses will identify themes whose wording does not fit some roles or disciplines.**  
Challenge by reviewing the relevant comments and subgroup pattern rather than assuming lack of relevance.

**H28. Open comments will reveal at least one important theme not represented in the 12 pairs.**  
Challenge through a transparent coding process that permits a genuine finding of thematic saturation.

**H29. Some paired items will not behave as a coherent contribution-versus-conditions pair.**  
Challenge by examining correlations, comments and respondent interpretation, especially the judgement pair.

**H30. The benchmark will change materially during early data collection before becoming more stable.**  
Challenge by preserving dated benchmark snapshots and examining the size of changes rather than assuming stability after an arbitrary response count.

## Recommended hypothesis log

Before analysing a new data cut, record:

| Field | What to record |
|---|---|
| Hypothesis ID | The identifier above |
| Status | Untested, supported, not supported, mixed or not testable |
| Analysis date | Date of the data cut |
| Eligible sample | Number included in the internal analysis |
| Missingness | Not applicable and prefer-not-to-say patterns |
| Evidence | Distribution, effect estimate, uncertainty and relevant comments |
| Alternative explanation | At least one credible competing interpretation |
| Decision | Retain, revise, reject or investigate qualitatively |
| Next test | The next analysis, interview or focused study needed |

This prevents an early pattern from quietly becoming a permanent claim and makes changes in interpretation visible as the research develops.

## Priority follow-up questions for later phases

The survey is best used to locate tensions. Follow-up work can then ask how and why they arise. Priority topics are likely to include:

- what happens to time saved by AI in practice;
- how teams decide when AI output requires checking or escalation;
- where formal AI policy differs from day-to-day behaviour;
- how AI changes recognition, authorship and professional identity;
- why some prototypes become embedded while others go nowhere;
- how leaders and individual contributors perceive the same organisational conditions differently; and
- which organisational practices allow AI adoption without sacrificing judgement, learning, relationships or sustainable pace.
